Factory Selection Guide

Choosing the right menstrual cup OEM partner determines product quality, regulatory readiness, and brand reputation. A factory that can mold silicone does not automatically have the material traceability, quality-system discipline, clean-area controls, packaging support, and batch documentation required for a professional menstrual-care brand.

This guide walks procurement teams through a seven-step evaluation covering material traceability, quality systems, production environment, customization capability, packaging support, QC checkpoints, and sample validation. It also provides comparison tables, timeline benchmarks, and a buyer FAQ so sourcing teams can ask better questions before issuing a purchase order.

The order and depth of review should match the quoted product, intended use, destination market, and buyer risk. A first-time DTC launch, a pharmacy retail program, and a distributor private-label range do not carry the same risk profile. Use the steps below as a framework, then define the evidence that matters for your project.

Procurement Process

Step 1: Verify the quoted material specification and traceability.

Request the quoted material specification, supplier and lot traceability, incoming acceptance criteria, and material change-control procedure. If a biocompatibility or other safety claim is required, identify the applicable test plan and verify that the report names the tested finished product or material, method, result, date, and scope. A material supplier file does not automatically prove the finished cup’s status.

For menstrual cups and discs, buyers usually ask about medical-grade silicone or other body-safe elastomers, cure system, catalyst type, colorants, and any additives. Ask whether the quoted material is the same material used in the sample, whether lot numbers can be traced from incoming silicone to molded batch, and how material substitutions are controlled. If the factory cannot explain its change-control process, the buyer has no reliable way to know whether a later production run matches the approved sample.

If biocompatibility testing such as ISO 10993 is part of the market plan, confirm the exact test matrix, sample identity, laboratory, method, and report scope. The report must cover the actual finished product or the material as used, not only a generic raw-material data sheet. Keep in mind that test reports have limitations: they support a technical file, but they do not replace the buyer’s own regulatory assessment.

Step 2: Review quality system certification scope.

If a quality-system certificate is relevant, verify the holder, legal entity, manufacturing address, standard, covered activities or products, issue date, expiry date, and current status. Buyers often ask about ISO 9001 and ISO 13485 . The certificate must match the legal entity and site that will actually mold, inspect, and pack the quoted product. A certificate for a trading company, parent company, or different factory address is not the same as site-specific manufacturing evidence.

For the United States, determine the product classification, code, operator duties, and any exemption limitations. Establishment registration or device listing is not FDA approval . Buyers should review FDA registration guidance with their regulatory counsel and confirm the route before making claims. For the European Union, determine the applicable legislation before using CE marking . CE marking must not be used when no covering rule requires it, and the manufacturer or authorized representative must be correctly identified in the technical documentation.

Use the FDA registration guidance and EU CE guidance as starting points, then confirm the route with the responsible regulatory parties. This guide does not provide legal advice, and YUJI does not promise that any certificate , registration, or mark is available for a given project. YUJI’s document availability must be confirmed per project.

Step 3: Assess production-environment controls.

Map molding, demolding, inspection, and packaging steps, then define the environmental and contamination controls required by the product risk and agreed quality plan. Menstrual cups are intimate-contact products. Even when a product is not manufactured in a classified cleanroom, the factory should be able to explain area separation, airflow, garmenting, hand hygiene, equipment cleaning, waste flow, and how open product is protected before packaging.

If a supplier claims a classified clean area, verify the exact site, room, standard, classification, monitoring method, test date, and operations covered. A generic facility photo or company-level certificate is not product-specific proof. Ask for current environmental monitoring records, particle and microbial limits where applicable, and corrective actions for out-of-limit results. Also confirm whether demolding, trimming, inspection, and bagging occur in the same controlled area or in different rooms, because the process map affects contamination risk.

Step 4: Evaluate size range, hardness, and customization capability.

Request quoted dimensions, verified capacity data, product-feel or measured hardness requirements, stem options, color controls, and logo methods. Confirm final values on approved samples and the specification revision; do not treat a generic range as evidence for the ordered SKU. A published size chart may be useful for catalog planning, but the buyer should verify the actual dimensions and capacity for the specific cup or disc being quoted.

Hardness affects insertion, comfort, seal, and removal. Ask whether hardness is specified by Shore value, by product-feel standard, or by both. If the factory offers multiple stem types, rim shapes, or suction-hole designs, request drawings and samples for each option. For color, clarify masterbatch source, color tolerance, lot-to-lot variation, and whether the colorant is suitable for the intended market. For logos, ask about pad printing, etching, embossing, or other methods, then confirm durability and appearance on the approved sample.

Customization can also include new molds, new sizes, or ODM development. Buyers often discuss new custom molds at 500+ units to amortize tooling, but this is an industry-typical benchmark only. The actual threshold depends on mold complexity, cavitation, material, and commercial terms. YUJI’s project-specific MOQ and tooling plan must be confirmed in the quotation.

Step 5: Review packaging and labeling support.

Packaging affects user perception, retail readiness, and regulatory compliance. Assess whether the factory can provide or source pouches, retail boxes, instruction leaflets, starter kits, and cartons. Request packaging examples from current private-label clients and confirm the artwork approval process, revision round limits, and lead time impact of custom packaging.

Ask whether the factory can handle retail-ready folding cartons, flexible pouches, printed labels, QR codes, batch codes, expiry or lot fields, and market-specific symbols. For Amazon, DTC, pharmacy, retail, or distributor channels, packaging requirements differ. A pharmacy buyer may need specific label fields and tamper evidence, while a DTC brand may prioritize unboxing and inserts. Confirm who owns artwork approval, who checks regulatory statements, and who is responsible if a claim on the pack is not accepted in the destination market.

Industry-typical benchmarks often place custom printed cartons or pouches at 500+ units per design , while pads and liners may require much higher volumes, such as around 300,000 pcs per SKU at one China factory. These figures are not YUJI’s quotation. YUJI’s packaging MOQ and lead time must be confirmed per project.

Step 6: Inspect QC checkpoints and batch release criteria.

Request the proposed in-process inspection frequency, dimensional tolerances, visual defect rules, applicable sterilization records, final release criteria, and record-retention responsibility. If the supplier cannot provide an approved example or explain confidentiality limits and an alternative review route, record the gap before selection.

A useful QC review should cover incoming material inspection, molding parameters, first-article approval, in-process dimensional checks, visual inspection for flash, tears, bubbles, foreign matter, and color variation, plus final packaging checks. For sterilized products, ask for sterilization method, validation status, cycle records, bioburden controls, and release criteria. For non-sterile products, confirm the cleaning and bioburden assumptions that support the label and intended use.

Batch release should be documented. Ask what makes a batch acceptable, who signs the release, what records are retained, and how long they are kept. If the buyer needs a batch QC report sample, request a redacted example. If confidentiality prevents sharing, ask for a live review during a factory visit or video audit. Record any missing evidence in the supplier risk file before placing the order.

Step 7: Confirm sample process and evaluation criteria.

Ask about sample lead time, cost, revision rounds, test responsibilities, and how approval is documented. Evaluate agreed fields such as surface finish, seam line, dimensions, hardness, odor, and packaging fit. An approved sample is an acceptance baseline, not a guarantee of mass-production conformity.

Industry-typical sampling benchmarks include catalog samples in 7–14 days , paid sample sets of 3–10 pieces , often shipped in 7–15 business days , and prototype-mold samples in 15–25 days . These are planning examples only. The actual sample schedule depends on material availability, mold status, color, packaging, and testing. YUJI’s sample lead time and sample fee must be confirmed per project.

Document the approval process. Which sample revision is approved? Who signs? What dimensions are measured? What hardness is accepted? What visual defects are allowed or rejected? If the sample is approved by photo only, state the limitations. If the buyer plans clinical or user trials, define the sample quantity and traceability needs early.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most important factor when choosing a menstrual cup OEM factory?

Material identity and traceability are critical. Request the quoted material specification, supplier and lot traceability, change-control process, and any finished-product test report needed for the intended use; verify that each document actually covers the quoted product. A factory that cannot trace material from incoming lot to molded batch creates quality and regulatory uncertainty.

Should I visit the factory before placing a menstrual cup OEM order?

Factory visits are recommended for first-time buyers planning volume orders. If a visit is not possible, request a video walkthrough of the injection molding area, QC inspection station, and packaging room, plus live QC records from a recent production batch. Ask to see how open product is protected, how molds are maintained, and how batch release is signed.

What sample quantities should I request for evaluation?

Use 5–10 samples per size only as a planning example. Define the sample count from the dimensions, physical checks, packaging fit, color review, intended evaluation, and any testing plan, then document acceptance before production. If multiple hardness, stem, or color options are offered, request a matrix of samples so the comparison is meaningful.

How long does the factory evaluation process take?

Use 2–4 weeks only as a planning example. Set the review schedule from document availability, sample shipment, testing, factory verification, corrective actions, and packaging approval; do not issue the purchase order until agreed gaps are closed. New molds, custom packaging, and regulatory questions can extend the timeline.

What red flags should I watch for in a menstrual cup factory audit?

Red flags include mismatched legal entities or addresses, expired or out-of-scope files, reports that do not identify the tested sample or method, refusal to define QC acceptance criteria, and pressure to approve artwork or production before samples and specifications are accepted. Also watch for vague answers about material substitution, clean-area claims, or batch record retention.

What MOQ should I plan for a menstrual cup OEM project?

MOQ varies by product, material, color, packaging, and tooling. Industry-typical catalog OEM cups and discs often start around 500 units per SKU , while custom printed cartons or pouches often require 500+ units per design . New custom molds are usually discussed at 500+ units to amortize tooling. These are benchmarks only. YUJI’s MOQ must be confirmed per quotation.

What certifications should I ask about?

Buyers commonly ask about ISO 9001 , ISO 13485 , ISO 10993 , FDA establishment registration , CE technical file support , REACH/RoHS , UKCA , and TGA . Verify the exact scope, holder, site, dates, and market route. These are industry-common requirements, not YUJI claims. YUJI’s document availability must be confirmed per project.

How should I evaluate packaging and labeling support?

Review pouch, retail box, leaflet, starter kit, and carton capability. Ask for current private-label packaging examples, artwork approval steps, revision round limits, and lead-time impact. Confirm who checks market-specific label claims and whether the factory can manage batch codes, lot fields, QR codes, and retail-ready cartons. Packaging affects both user perception and regulatory readiness.
