## Title

How to Compare Menstrual Cup OEM Manufacturers Before Sampling

## Subtitle

A procurement-first checklist for comparing material documentation, design choices, trial-order readiness, packaging, and market responsibilities—before a private-label launch becomes expensive to change.

## Medium tags

`Manufacturing` · `Supply Chain` · `Product Sourcing` · `Private Label` · `Feminine Care`

## Article

Choosing a menstrual cup OEM manufacturer is not a matter of collecting the lowest unit price from a catalog. For a feminine-care brand, Amazon seller, pharmacy buyer, retailer, or distributor, the more useful question is: *Can this supplier turn the same written brief into a repeatable product, pack, document set, and handoff process?*

That question should be answered before sampling—not after artwork, listings, and launch dates have already been committed.

This checklist is designed for comparing manufacturers on evidence, clarity, and execution. It is not a substitute for legal, regulatory, or product-safety advice for a specific market.

### 1. Start with a material specification, not a marketing phrase

“Silicone” is a starting point, not a complete purchasing specification. Ask each supplier to identify the material grade or specification it proposes for the exact cup, the relevant supplier or batch-identification approach, the colour system, and any available documentation for that material. Keep the request tied to the SKU and revision you are evaluating.

Avoid turning a generic brochure into a product claim. A material declaration, a supplier document, and a finished-product test report are different things. Ask what is available, what relates to the proposed configuration, who issued it, what date or revision it carries, and whether the intended destination market requires any additional review. If a document is unavailable, that is a commercial fact to record—not a gap to fill with assumptions.

This matters because product treatment differs by market. For example, the U.S. FDA’s classification database lists a menstrual cup as a Class II device under product code HHE, while market obligations elsewhere may follow different frameworks. The correct outcome of this comparison is a documented question list for the importer and its advisers, not a supplier’s blanket compliance promise.

### 2. Put hardness, dimensions, and design into a controlled comparison sheet

Two cups can look similar in a photo and behave very differently as a product programme. Before requesting samples, define the fields that must be comparable across every quotation:

- material description and proposed colour;
- hardness target or range, including the measurement method if it is available;
- diameter, body length, stem design, rim profile, and stated capacity;
- size range and the logic for the initial assortment;
- logo position, mould status, surface finish, and any custom-development request; and
- accessories included with the sample or proposed retail pack.

Do not ask a supplier to decide the end user’s needs for you. A supplier can explain available structures, but the brand should own its intended product position, instructions, and target-market review. A buyer comparing a pharmacy pilot with an Amazon bundle, for example, may need different pack contents, labelling, language, and carton planning even if the cup itself is unchanged.

Keep every decision in a dated specification sheet. If a drawing, sample, or artwork changes, mark the revision. That simple discipline prevents a common sourcing failure: approving a sample while pricing, packaging, and inspection teams are still working from an earlier version.

### 3. Treat samples as a decision gate, not a promotional giveaway

The purpose of a sample is to reduce uncertainty. A useful sample request names the cup structure, size, colour direction, logo status, pouch or box direction, instruction language, target market, and the questions the buyer intends to answer.

For each supplier, ask for the sample path in writing:

1. What is an existing structure versus a custom-development scope?
2. What exactly will be supplied for review—cup only, pouch, draft artwork, leaflet mock-up, or a complete mock retail pack?
3. Who approves product details, artwork, instructions, and claims before a trial order is released?
4. What changes after sample approval: price, MOQ, timing, packaging MOQ, or document scope?
5. How will the supplier identify the approved sample and carry that version into first-article and production checks?

The strongest comparison is not “which sample feels best?” It is “which supplier can connect an approved sample to a clear next-order specification without ambiguity?”

### 4. Compare private-label execution beyond the logo

Private label is an integrated package decision. A retail box, storage pouch, instruction leaflet, barcode, outer carton, and marketplace image set can each have separate lead times, minimums, approval owners, and language implications.

Ask suppliers to separate the product quote from the packaging and artwork assumptions. Then request a list of buyer-owned inputs: brand files, copy, translations, barcode ownership, country-of-origin information where applicable, carton marks, and final sign-off contacts. This makes it easier to compare a pouch-only trial with a more complex retail programme without treating them as the same SKU.

For a structured pre-sampling question list, see YUJI’s [menstrual cup OEM sourcing guide](https://yujihealth.com/resources/menstrual-cup-oem-sourcing-guide/). Use it to prepare questions; do not treat it as proof that any document, claim, or market requirement applies to every project.

### 5. Make MOQ, inspection, and documents quote-specific

MOQ is rarely one number. It can change with cup structure, size mix, colour, logo treatment, packaging components, artwork, documents, and the timing of the launch. A small trial order may be practical for one configuration and impractical for another.

Ask each manufacturer to state the assumptions behind its MOQ and to distinguish product MOQ from packaging-component minimums. Request the process for first-article approval, in-process sampling, final inspection, lot or batch identification where applicable, and release records. Do not assume that the word “QC” means the same process at every supplier.

For documents, list the exact items requested and their purpose: material specification, product specification, available certificate scans, inspection records, test summaries, packaging files, or other market-specific items. Ask whether each item is available for the proposed SKU, whether it is current, and whether it is supplied by the manufacturer, a material provider, or an independent laboratory. A document pack should be reviewed for relevance and authenticity by the party responsible for market placement; it should not be converted into a marketing statement automatically.

### 6. Assign destination-market and importer responsibilities early

A manufacturer can provide product and production information, but it does not replace the importer’s or brand owner’s responsibilities in the destination market. Before approving a trial order, name the market, selling channel, legal entity placing the product on the market, label languages, intended claims, traceability expectations, and required records.

In the EU, the General Product Safety Regulation describes responsibilities for economic operators, including importers’ duties to check that applicable requirements are met and that products carry the required information and instructions. In Great Britain, government guidance similarly states that manufacturers and importers must be able to demonstrate compliance with relevant requirements before placing products on the market. These are starting points for a market review, not a universal compliance checklist.

Build an owner matrix: the supplier owns production answers; the brand owns commercial decisions and approved claims; the importer or responsible local party owns market-placement obligations; and specialist advisers confirm jurisdiction-specific requirements. If no one owns a question, it is not ready for production.

### A practical comparison scorecard

Before selecting a supplier, score each candidate against the same six headings: specification clarity, sample traceability, packaging readiness, MOQ assumptions, inspection and document traceability, and destination-market responsibility mapping. Add a final column for unanswered questions and a named owner for each answer.

The best manufacturer for a launch is not necessarily the one that replies fastest or quotes the lowest unit price. It is the one whose proposed product, sample, packaging, inspection, documentation, and handoff can be understood—and approved—without hidden assumptions.

## CTA

Before requesting a quote, turn this article into a one-page supplier comparison sheet and send the same controlled brief to each shortlisted OEM. Ask for written assumptions, document scope, sample contents, and approval steps. That gives your team a basis for comparing responses before committing to a trial order.
